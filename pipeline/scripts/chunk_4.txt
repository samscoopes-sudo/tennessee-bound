But here's the catch the photos hide. Knoxville prices have moved fast. One market analysis this year clocked the Knoxville median at $327,000, up more than 20 percent in a single year. That's a college town filling up, and it's filling up partly with the exact same wave of retirees you're part of. The safe, sensible middle is exactly where everyone else is aiming too, which means the competition for the good house at the fair price is fiercest right there. And a university brings its own quiet costs — game-day traffic that shuts down whole neighborhoods on fall Saturdays, student-rental turnover on nearby streets. It's a fine choice. But "fine and popular" is not the same as "the deal you saw online," and the gap between those two is measured in tens of thousands of dollars.

Number six: the cheapest-sticker-price town. This is the one that gets people who are being careful with money — which, if you're watching this, is probably you. And these towns are real, so let me name them. In Paris, Tennessee, up near Kentucky Lake with its own 60-foot Eiffel Tower replica, home values sit around $215,000 — roughly $120,000 below the state average — according to WorldAtlas this year. Dyersburg runs about $165,000, and Humboldt around $177,500, according to market data compiled by ok.com. Go further and you'll find Trezevant, in Carroll County, at a 2025 median near $129,000, according to Home Stratosphere, and Savannah, down in Hardin County, with homes around $122,000. Your eye lights up at those numbers, because that's exactly what you came for.

But the sticker price on the house is only one line in a much longer bill, and these towns are cheap for reasons that cost you elsewhere. Look where they are. Dyersburg and Humboldt and Trezevant are out in rural West Tennessee — the same part of the state where Decatur County lost its hospital, where the rural-hospital math we already covered hits hardest. Paris sits in Henry County near the water, which means the flood-zone question from a few minutes ago is live for a lot of the prettiest lots. The cheap house and the thin healthcare and the flood risk are often the same address. And let me put real everyday numbers on the rest of it, because "cost of living" gets thrown around loosely. In Tennessee, groceries run around $302 to $320 a month per person, according to 2026 figures from SoFi and OysterLink. Utilities average roughly $160 to $180 a month. Gas is a bright spot — about $2.78 a gallon against a national average of $3.13, per ConsumerAffairs, because Tennessee's fuel tax is low. Overall the state runs about 10 to 13 percent below the national average on cost of living. That's a genuine advantage. But none of it changes based on how cheap your house was. The house is a one-time price. The groceries, the utilities, the gas to drive 40 minutes to the nearest ER — those repeat every month for the rest of your life.

Number seven ties them all together: the town that's perfect except for one line on the tax bill. Here's a number that sounds like great news, and mostly is. Tennessee's average effective property tax rate is about 0.56 percent, according to 2026 retirement analysis. On a $300,000 home in Knoxville, that's roughly $1,680 a year. Compare that to the same-value home in Texas at around $4,800, Illinois around $5,400, or New Jersey around $6,600. If you're coming from one of those states, that difference alone can be $4,000 or $5,000 a year back in your pocket. That's real. That's a big reason people make this move. But the catch is the sales tax, and it's the number the property-tax celebration always leaves out. Tennessee's combined state and local sales tax averages around 9.55 percent — one of the highest in the country — and while food is taxed at a lower rate, groceries are not fully exempt. So the state that saves you thousands on property tax quietly takes some of it back every time you buy anything. It nets out well for most retirees, especially coming from a high-tax state. But "nets out well" is a calculation you have to actually run for your own spending — not assume from a headline.
