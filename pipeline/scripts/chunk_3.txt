Here's the mechanic you need to understand. If your beautiful lake house sits in a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area — that's a Zone A or Zone AE on the flood map — and you carry a mortgage, your lender will require separate flood insurance on top of everything else. FEMA's own guidance is blunt about it: a home in that zone has a 26 percent chance of flooding during the life of a 30-year mortgage. That's better than one in four. The average flood policy through the National Flood Insurance Program in Tennessee runs around $1,220 a year, according to a Tennessee agency's 2026 figures — another bill the listing never mentioned. And even away from the water, watch the deductible. On a percentage-based wind deductible, two percent on a $350,000 home means you personally pay the first $7,000 of any storm claim before insurance pays a dime, according to MoneyGeek. The good news, and I'll come back to this, is that you can check any address for free on FEMA's Flood Map Service Center before you ever make an offer. Five minutes. Government data.

Number four, and this is the one that worries me the most: the pretty little town in the county with no hospital. This is where the online picture is the most dangerous of all, because health care access doesn't show up in a real estate photo. It can't. And I'm done being vague about it, so let me name names. In April 2020, Decatur County lost its only hospital, and today the nearest emergency room is about 17 miles away in Lexington, in the next county over, according to reporting from Fox 17. That's the town I opened with — Fairview — which had no ER at all until May 2026. Those aren't rare exceptions. Since 2010, Tennessee has lost inpatient care in 16 rural communities — second worst in the entire country, behind only Texas — according to the Chartis 2025 rural health report. Twenty Tennessee counties have no hospital at all, and 17 of those also have no emergency department whatsoever, according to the Sycamore Institute.

And it's not just missing buildings. The Sycamore Institute names specific counties among the very worst in the state for how many residents there are per primary care doctor — Bledsoe, Lauderdale, and Marshall counties, along with Hancock, Macon, and Trousdale. Those are the towns that look like a painting in the thumbnail. And here's the hard truth: the charm and the risk come from the very same source. There just aren't many people out there. That's what makes it quiet and green and cheap, and that's exactly what makes it impossible to keep a hospital open. When you're 68 years old, the distance to an emergency room is not a lifestyle preference or a minor inconvenience. It's the whole game. A house in Bledsoe County can be flawless in every single photo and still sit 30 or 40 minutes from the nearest place that can treat a stroke, where the difference between 12 minutes and 40 minutes is the difference between walking out of the hospital and not.
