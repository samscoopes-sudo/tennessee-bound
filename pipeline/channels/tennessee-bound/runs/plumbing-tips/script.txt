Let us start with the one I get called out for more than anything else. Clogged drains. Now here is what most people do wrong. They reach for that bottle of chemical drain cleaner, the stuff that promises to dissolve everything. Do not use it. I cannot say that strongly enough. Those chemicals eat through your pipes over time, especially if you have older cast iron or PVC. What you want instead is a simple drain snake. You can pick one up for fifteen dollars at any hardware store. Push it down, twist it, and pull. Nine times out of ten, you are going to pull out a clump of hair and soap scum, and that drain is going to flow like new. I have seen homeowners spend three hundred dollars on a plumber visit for something that took me forty-five seconds with a snake.

Now here is where most people get into real trouble, and it is not where you would expect. Your water heater. That big tank sitting in your basement or garage, quietly doing its job, until one day it is not. Most folks never think about it until they are standing in a cold shower. But here is the thing most people do not know. There is a little valve on the bottom of your water heater called the drain valve, and once a year you need to flush it. Sediment builds up in there, minerals from your water supply, and it settles on the bottom like sand. That layer of gunk makes your heater work twice as hard, drives your energy bill up, and cuts the life of the unit in half. Hook up a garden hose, open the valve, let it run for about five minutes until the water comes out clear. That ten-minute job can add five to eight years to a water heater that costs over a thousand dollars to replace.

Alright, number three, and this is the one I told you about at the top. The one thing I wish every single homeowner knew. Know where your main water shutoff valve is. I cannot tell you how many times I have walked into a house where water is spraying everywhere, the ceiling is coming down, the floors are buckling, and the homeowner is standing there in a panic asking me to fix it. And the first thing I do every single time is walk to the shutoff valve and turn it off. That is it. The flooding stops. But here is what kills me. If they had known where that valve was, they could have stopped the damage in the first thirty seconds instead of waiting forty-five minutes for me to show up. Most shutoff valves are near the front of the house where the main water line comes in, or in the basement near the water meter. Go find yours right now. Seriously, pause this video and go look. Knowing that one thing could save you ten, twenty, even fifty thousand dollars in water damage.

Now let me tell you about something that drives me absolutely crazy, and I see it in almost every house I walk into. Overtightening. People think tighter means better, and I get it, that makes sense in your head. But with plumbing fittings, overtightening is one of the fastest ways to crack a fitting or strip a thread. And a cracked fitting behind a wall is a nightmare you do not want. Here is the rule I teach every apprentice. Hand-tight, then a quarter turn with a wrench. That is it. If you are white-knuckling a wrench and grunting, you have gone way too far. Plumbing connections seal with the thread tape or the rubber washer, not with brute force. A gentle hand saves you from a leak you will not find until the drywall is already ruined.

Speaking of tape, let us talk about the most misunderstood product in plumbing. Teflon tape, or thread seal tape, that thin white stuff you wrap around pipe threads. Most people wrap it the wrong direction. I am not joking, this is probably the number one mistake I see from DIY plumbers. You need to wrap it clockwise when you are looking at the end of the pipe, so that when you screw the fitting on, the tape tightens into the threads instead of bunching up and unraveling. Wrap it around three or four times, keep it tight against the threads, and overlap by about half the width of the tape. Do it right and you have a watertight seal every time. Do it backwards and you are going to be scratching your head wondering why it still drips.

Now here is one that sounds simple but has a trick to it that most people miss. Fixing a running toilet. A running toilet can waste two hundred gallons of water a day, which adds up fast on your water bill. Most of the time, the problem is the flapper, that rubber piece at the bottom of the tank. They wear out, they warp, and they stop sealing properly. You can replace one in about five minutes for less than ten dollars. But here is the part most people skip. Before you buy a new flapper, run your finger along the rim of the flush valve, that is the ring the flapper sits on. If it is rough, pitted, or has mineral buildup, no flapper in the world is going to seal against it. Take some fine sandpaper or a Scotch-Brite pad and smooth that surface out first. That one extra step is the difference between a fix that lasts and one that has you back at the hardware store next month.

But you know what, not everything is a fix. Sometimes the smartest plumbing move is prevention, and this next one is something I started doing in my own house years ago. Mesh drain screens. They cost about two dollars each, you set them over your shower drain and kitchen sink drain, and they catch all the debris before it goes down. Hair, food scraps, soap chunks, all of it. I clean mine out once a week, takes about ten seconds, and I have not had a clogged drain in my own house in over a decade. Two dollars versus a three hundred dollar plumber visit. That is the kind of math I like.

Now here is one that can actually be dangerous if you get it wrong, so pay attention. If you ever smell rotten eggs near a sink or drain you do not use very often, that is sewer gas coming up through a dry P-trap. Every drain in your house has a curved pipe underneath called a P-trap, and it holds a small amount of water that acts as a seal against sewer gases. If you have a guest bathroom or a basement drain you never use, that water evaporates over time and the seal breaks. The fix is dead simple. Just run the water for about thirty seconds every month in every drain you do not use regularly. That refills the trap and keeps those gases where they belong, which is underground and not in your living room.

Let me give you one more that most people never think about until it is too late. Know what you can and cannot put down a garbage disposal. I have pulled some truly unbelievable things out of disposals over the years. But the three biggest offenders are grease, potato peels, and pasta. Grease coats the inside of your pipes and hardens like candle wax. Potato peels turn into a starchy paste that gums up the blades. And pasta expands with water, so even after you grind it up, it keeps swelling inside your pipes. The rule is simple. If you would not eat it raw, do not put it down the disposal. And always run cold water, not hot, while the disposal is running. Cold water keeps any grease solid so it gets chopped up instead of melting and coating your pipes.

And the last one, and this is something that has saved me personally more times than I can count. Before you start any plumbing project, any project at all, take a picture with your phone. Take a picture of how everything is connected before you take it apart. Which pipe goes where, which fitting faces which direction, how the supply lines are routed. I have been doing this for twenty-five years and I still do it every single time. Because when you are elbow deep under a sink with water dripping on your face, your memory is not as good as you think it is. That photo takes two seconds and it has saved me hours of frustration.
